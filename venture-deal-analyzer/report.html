The Evidence-Based Deal Analyzer

What sixty years of decision science, the VC returns literature, and the 2025–26 LLM-evaluation research say about building a deal-analysis system that is fast, sector-flexible, and provably improving.

Page-one synthesis — the format every memo will open with
Build it — as a disqualifier of predictably-bad deals and a detector of outlier upside, wrapped in a forecast ledger that makes the methodology provable on our own book.
  • The power law dictates the design. ~65% of deals return <1x and ~6% of deals produce ~60% of returns. At seed, a missed winner costs more than any number of small losses — so the analyzer's two verdicts are a kill-list score and an outlier thesis, never a single "rating."
  • Structure is the biggest free win. Mechanical combination of structured scores beats holistic gut across 136 studies; structured interviews predict at r≈.42 vs r≈.19 for the unstructured meetings VCs actually run.
  • Self-improvement works, but not by re-running one deal. Repeat runs measure consistency, not correctness — a judge can be perfectly repeatable and consistently wrong. The engine is a golden set of resolved deals plus a prospective probability ledger scored over time.

01 · The shape of the problem

Venture returns are a power law, so analysis errors are asymmetric

Across 21,640 US financings (Correlation Ventures, 2004–2013), most deals lose money and virtually all the return lives in a thin tail. The best funds don't have fewer misses — Horsley Bridge data shows top funds hold more sub-1x deals than average funds, and win on bigger hits.

02 · The judgment layer

Structure beats gut — and the industry runs on gut

The most robust finding in all of selection science: mechanically combining structured scores outperforms holistic expert judgment (Meehl/Grove, 136-study meta-analysis; clinical judgment wins in only 6–16% of studies). Yet the modal VC founder meeting is an unstructured conversation — close to the worst validated assessment method.

And weight the horse, not just the jockey. VCs say team is everything (47% rank it #1; 96% attribute success to it). Outcome data disagrees: from business plan to IPO, business lines almost never change while management usually does (Kaplan-Sensoy-Strömberg). The scorecard gives market/business parity with team — and the founder module scores what the evidence supports: execution track record (+9pp for a prior exit), team trait diversity, equity-split process, and conflict red flags (65% of high-potential failures are people problems) — not charisma, not youth (mean age of the fastest-growing 0.1% of founders: 45), and not grit scales (a jangle-fallacy rebrand of conscientiousness).

Cross-sector, the non-founder evidence hierarchy is: timing (industry-vintage effects are enormous: 52% of 1983-founded computer startups IPO'd vs 18% of the 1985 cohort) > market elasticity (top-down TAM rejected Uber by ~15x; require bottom-up builds) > moat trajectory (at seed only counter-positioning and cornered resources are assessable — score the sequence to scale/network/switching power, not today's moat) > entry price (across a 5x seed valuation spread, no difference in outcomes — price is an ownership input at seed, a first-order driver only at B+).

04 · The self-improvement loop

Re-running one deal is a gate, not an engine

The question on the table: is running the analyzer on the same deal over and over a smart self-improvement strategy? The literature splits it exactly in half:

✓ As a reliability gate — yes, mandatory

  • LLM judges give different scores to identical inputs across runs ("Rating Roulette," 2025).
  • Run each deal 7–10 times, report median + IQR; alert when spread exceeds one rubric point.
  • A wide spread usually indicts the rubric, not the deal — that's the useful signal.

✗ As a training signal — no, provably insufficient

  • Consistency ≠ accuracy: judges with high test-retest reliability still carry severe systematic bias — repeatable and wrong ("Reliability without Validity," 2026).
  • Agreement with itself contains zero information about outcomes.
  • Sycophancy is systematic: framed bullish, the model drifts bullish — and re-running amplifies the same bias 10 times.

The actual engine has three parts, in increasing order of value:

  • Golden set — 150–300 resolved deals from our own pipeline (invested and passed, including dead companies), anonymized so the model can't recognize famous outcomes from training data, labeled with fast proxies (next round ≤24mo, alive at 3y). Split dev / held-out / temporal holdout; iterate prompts only against dev.
  • Bias battery on every prompt change — position-swap, verbosity (padded vs terse memo), sycophancy (bullish/bearish/neutral framing); pass = deltas inside the reliability noise band.
  • Prospective forecast ledger — every live deal logs P(next round ≤24mo), P(<1x), P(≥10x) at decision time; scored with Brier + pairwise rank correlation per prompt version. Contamination-proof by construction and compounds forever. This is where "proven methodology" stops being a claim and becomes a chart.

Honesty box
  • Founder-personality evidence is correlational, on VC-visible survivor samples; classifier accuracies don't transfer to our deal flow at face value.
  • Rebel Fund's backtested IRR and ghSMART's hit rates are self-published/vendor-reported — treated as existence proofs only.
  • LLM-vs-human results cover screening, not allocation or check sizing; datasets skew toward observable signals.
  • Any backtest on famous deals is contaminated — the model already knows Airbnb won. Valid backtests need anonymized or post-cutoff deals.
  • VC return persistence is substantially deal access, not judgment (early lucky wins compound into better deal flow). This system improves judgment and error rates; it does not manufacture access.
  • The 42%-timing figure is retrospective and subjectively coded; the NFX 70% network-effects figure comes from a network-effects-thesis fund. Directional, both.
