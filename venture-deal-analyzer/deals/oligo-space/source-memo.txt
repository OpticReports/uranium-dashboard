Oligo Space | Memo
Oligo Space is building the manufacturing infrastructure that will allow enterprises to own application-specific orbital
infrastructure without becoming space companies themselves. The company combines AI-native spacecraft design,
vertically integrated manufacturing, testing and operations to make customized satellites faster and cheaper than
traditional aerospace manufacturers.
The core thesis is simple: launch has become cheap and abundant; satellite manufacturing and mission
design are now the bottleneck. Oligo is building the production system for the next phase of the space
economy.



Key Investment Highlights

    ●​ Existing manufacturers optimize for either customization or scale, but struggle to deliver both simultaneously.
    ●​ Oligo is attacking the manufacturing process itself, using AI to reduce the engineering burden associated
       with customization.
    ●​ Vertical integration gives Oligo control over the design-to-manufacturing loop.

The Opportunity

Space is undergoing a structural transition from government-led, bespoke aerospace programs to commercial,
application-specific orbital infrastructure.
Launch costs have fallen dramatically. Falcon 9 reduced the cost of reaching Low Earth Orbit (LEO) by >95% versus
the Space Shuttle, while Starship targets sub-$100/kg economics. Global orbital launches increased from 87 in 2015
to 259 in 2024 and >320 in 2025. Active satellites increased from ~1,000-1,400 a decade ago to >13,000 by late
2025.
The industry is moving from “Can we get something into space?” to “What should we put into space for a
specific business?”
The launch and manufacturing represent only 7% of the $613B global space economy, while 93% flows through
applications, services and ground infrastructure. The application layer determines whether infrastructure gets built.
Enterprises can either rent data/capacity or own the means of production. A 1997 GAO analysis found that the DoD
could meet needs 14x more cheaply by owning capacity rather than leasing it. As costs fall, ownership becomes
accessible to enterprises with persistent or proprietary demand.
Oligo addresses the gap between buying generic satellite data/services and building an expensive, slow
internal aerospace program.



The Oligo System: Product, Advantage, and Moat

Oligo is effectively building the infrastructure layer that lets an enterprise define a mission and own the spacecraft
infrastructure required to execute it. This vertical integration spans the entire lifecycle: business requirement →
spacecraft architecture → AI-assisted design → manufacturing → testing → launch → operations → data.
Oligo’s AI-driven processes are designed to compress spacecraft development timelines from years to months, while
reducing costs from traditional tens of millions of dollars to a fraction of that. Oligo is not an aerospace software
company with a manufacturing component; it is building an AI-native physical manufacturing system where software
directly improves the economics of producing spacecraft.
Oligo wins by offering a fundamentally better manufacturing system than existing project-based models. Most of the
~120 satellite manufacturers remain engineering-heavy; every new customer requires substantial human involvement
in architecture, simulation, component selection, integration, and testing. This creates a linear relationship where
more customers require more engineering headcount.
In contrast, Oligo is building Zenith, an AI-native system that turns spacecraft engineering into a repeatable
production process. Zenith assists with mission requirements, architectures, design evaluation, and optimization. The
real moat is the closed-loop manufacturing data flywheel. Every spacecraft Oligo produces generates data across
Design → Simulation → Manufacturing → Testing → Flight → Performance. This feedback loop ensures that each
spacecraft makes the next one faster to design, cheaper to manufacture, easier to test, and more reliable.
Over time, Oligo builds a proprietary dataset connecting physical design and manufacturing outcomes to in-orbit
performance. The company does not need to be just another satellite manufacturer; it aims to be the production
platform that makes customized spacecraft manufacturing economically scalable.



Why Now

Launch is no longer the primary bottleneck. Reusable launch has fundamentally changed space economics and
increased orbital access.
Satellite hardware is becoming standardized. The Space Development Agency’s order for 126 identical buses is a
strong signal that mass production is becoming the expected model.
There is already demand building up visible in the following sectors:


    ●​ Financial: Hedge funds spent $2.8B on alternative data in 2025, including satellite imagery for market pricing.
    ●​ Manufacturing: Varda is using microgravity for pharmaceutical manufacturing and has raised $329M.
    ●​ RF: Deloitte is flying nine satellites for RF monitoring.
    ●​ Mining / Energy: Satellite data is used for deposit location and methane leak detection.
    ●​ Automotive: Geely is investing $637M in a vehicle-connectivity constellation.
    ●​ Agriculture / Consumer: Nestlé, Cargill and Unilever use space for crop-yield forecasting and deforestation
       verification.
    ●​ Insurance: AXA and Munich Re use satellite data to trigger insurance policies.
    ●​ Connectivity: Apple has committed $1.95B to Globalstar for direct satellite connectivity.

Accenture reports that 80% of executives view space technology as vital for growth, but only 18% have integrated it.
This suggests significant adoption headroom.
Global telecom operators alone spent $299.4B in capex in 2024, roughly 38x the total venture funding deployed into
space that year. Companies such as Amazon and AT&T operate at a scale where annual capex plans dwarf the
space startup ecosystem of the past decade.
Much of the corporate capital is currently flowing into AI infrastructure. As AI capex eventually normalizes,
enterprises may seek another strategic frontier for large-scale investment. Space could become a major next
corporate spending cycle because orbital data, connectivity and infrastructure provide capabilities that cannot simply
be replicated on Earth.
Competitive Landscape

 Category                      Customization                Repeatability               Enterprise fit
 Legacy defense primes         High                         Low                         Low
 Commercial bus builders       Low                          High                        Medium
 Data / service providers      None                         High                        High
 Space infrastructure giants   Medium                       High                        Medium
 Oligo Space                   High                         High                        High

Legacy defense primes: Lockheed Martin and Northrop Grumman offer high customization but low repeatability,
with cost-plus structures and multi-year timelines.
Commercial platform builders: Apex, Rocket Lab and Terran Orbital offer repeatability but require missions to fit
pre-cataloged hardware.
Data and service operators: Planet and Spire sell generic data feeds, leaving customers exposed to perpetual
margins and data-rights limitations.
Infrastructure and utility giants: SpaceX's Starshield adapts standardized buses for government payloads, while
Blue Origin's Blue Ring focuses on orbital utility services.
Oligo’s target quadrant is customization + repeatability.

Conclusion

Oligo Space is a bet on the next manufacturing layer of the space economy. Declining launch costs and standardized
satellite platforms have created a new bottleneck: how enterprises efficiently design and manufacture spacecraft
optimized for their specific applications.
Oligo can win not by competing head-on with every satellite manufacturer, but by changing the economics of
customization itself. If it converts spacecraft engineering from a labor-intensive project business into an AI-driven,
repeatable manufacturing process, its advantage should compound with every spacecraft it builds.
Bottom line: Oligo offers a differentiated way to invest in the transition from “space as a service” to “space
as enterprise-owned infrastructure.”
