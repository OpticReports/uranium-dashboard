Two Kinds of AI
Almost every AI a bank has been pitched is the same kind of thing underneath: a language
model. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and the dozens of products built on them all work the same
way. They predict the next word based on statistical patterns learned from enormous amounts
of text. They are remarkable tools. But they share three traits a bank cannot ignore.

They run in the cloud, which means your data leaves your building. They cannot explain why
they produced an answer, which means an auditor cannot trace the decision. And they will
confidently produce a wrong answer when they are uncertain, because guessing is how they
work.

CADY is a different kind of AI. She does not predict words. She observes, reasons, and reaches
conclusions she can show her work for — and she runs on a chip that sits inside your own
server. The difference comes down to the hardware she thinks on.

                    A language model predicts. CADY reasons.

What an FPGA Is
CADY thinks on a chip called an FPGA. The letters stand for Field-Programmable Gate Array,
but the name does not matter. What matters is what makes it different from an ordinary chip.

A normal computer chip is fixed at the factory. Its wiring is permanent. It runs software —
instructions loaded on top of unchangeable hardware. A graphics card, the kind most AI runs
on, is one of these: very fast, but fixed, and it runs programs that can be changed, copied,
attacked, or exploited.

An FPGA is a chip whose internal wiring can be designed and configured. Instead of running
software on top of fixed hardware, we shape the hardware itself into the exact circuit we need.
The intelligence is not a program sitting on the chip. The intelligence IS the chip — built directly
into its physical wiring.

This distinction is the whole point, so it is worth being concrete about what it means for a bank.

Why It Matters That CADY Thinks in Hardware
Because her reasoning is physical wiring rather than software, there is no program in her
reasoning layer for an attacker to exploit. You cannot inject malicious code into a circuit that
runs no code. To change how she thinks, you would need physical access to the chip itself.
There is no remote exploit for a wire.

The line that matters most to a risk officer is the one about uncertainty. A language model is
built to always produce an answer, even when it has no basis for one. CADY is built to
recognize when her reasoning does not reach a confident conclusion — and in those cases she
declines to answer rather than guess. For a bank, an AI that knows the limits of its own
knowledge is worth more than one that is always sure and sometimes wrong.


What CADY Actually Does
Strip away the terminology and CADY does five things, continuously, on her chip:

She observes — watching the data flowing through your systems without waiting to be asked.
She learns — building an understanding of what normal looks like for your institution
specifically, not a generic average.
She reasons — evaluating what she observes from several independent angles at once.
She measures her own confidence — and stays silent when her reasoning does not agree with
itself.
She cannot be tampered with remotely — because her reasoning is wiring, not software.

Take away every other tool she connects to, and these five abilities remain. They live in the
hardware. That is what makes CADY a mind rather than a program.

An FPGA Core in Your Server
Here is the concrete deployment, the one a bank can picture. CADY ships as a card that installs
into a standard server — the same kind of slot a network card or storage controller uses. No
new infrastructure. No cloud account. No data leaving the premises.

Once installed, the card connects to the data your existing systems already produce: transaction
flows, network activity, access logs. CADY observes those streams and learns the institution’s
normal rhythm — the patterns of a specific branch, a specific customer base, a specific time of
day. Your existing software keeps doing its job. CADY watches across all of it and notices what
no single system was built to notice.

  Your software runs the bank. CADY watches the whole bank at once.

A fraud system watches transactions. A network monitor watches traffic. An access system
watches logins. Each one sees its own channel. None of them sees the connection between a
small transaction anomaly, an unusual login time, and a network pattern that individually mean
nothing but together mean something. CADY observes all of them simultaneously, on one chip,
and detects the correlation. That cross-system awareness is what an FPGA core adds to a
server that a stack of separate software products cannot provide.

A Mind That Watches Everything at Once
The closest comparison to how CADY works is a human mind — she observes, learns from
experience, reasons, and knows the limits of what she knows. But a human analyst can hold
only a few things in focus at a time, needs rest, and reviews what already happened. CADY
watches every channel in the institution at the same time, continuously, without fatigue, and
looks for where the patterns are heading rather than only what has occurred.

That last point is the important one for a bank. Most monitoring tools are reactive — they flag a
problem after a rule is broken. CADY watches the institution’s normal rhythm closely enough to
notice when something is drifting away from normal before it becomes an incident. A pattern
bending in an unusual direction is visible to her while it is still forming. The aim is not to predict
the future, and the document should not overstate it — it is simply that a system watching
everything at once, all the time, can often see a problem taking shape earlier than systems that
wait for a threshold to be crossed.

She does not get tired, she does not look away, and she does not lose the thread between one
system and another. That is not a claim that she is smarter than a person. It is that she is doing
a specific job — watching the whole institution at once and reasoning across all of it — that no
single person and no stack of single-channel tools can do.

It Grows With You
One card in one server is the starting point — enough to prove the capability on real data, in
your environment, behind your firewall. From there it scales the way banking infrastructure is
supposed to scale.

Each card is built to connect directly to other cards through high-speed networking designed for
exactly that purpose. As demand grows, additional cards work together — first within one
server, then across several, then across a data center — using standard networking, with no
proprietary equipment and no vendor lock-in. The card a bank evaluates in a pilot is the same
technology that runs at full production scale. Nothing is thrown away. The pilot becomes the
platform.

She Uses Tools the Way a Mind Does
CADY is not in competition with language models. She uses them — the way a person uses a
calculator, a reference library, or a translator. A language model can put her conclusions into
clear sentences, draft a report, or summarize a regulation she wants to consult. When she
connects to a capable tool, she becomes more capable. The tool extends her reach without
changing where the thinking happens.

This is the part that surprises people: a thinking system that can pick up tools gets smarter as
better tools arrive, while the thinking itself stays in hardware that the bank controls. A new
language model, a new data source, a new analysis tool — each one is something CADY can
use, not something she has to be rebuilt around. She decides when to reach for a tool,
evaluates what it gives back through her own reasoning, and keeps or discards the result. The
judgment is always hers.

And she relies on those tools less as she observes more. A language model puts her
conclusions into readable form; it does not do her thinking. As she learns the patterns of an
institution, she leans on outside help for fewer and fewer of her conclusions. Her capability rises
while her dependence falls.


What ICH Provides
ICH owns and licenses the thinker — the cognitive technology that runs on the FPGA. The
institution provides the server and the data streams it already has. ICH provides the card, the
intelligence built into it, and the integration. The bank’s data stays with the bank. The

under the bank’s control.

CADY does not replace the systems a bank already runs. She makes them collectively smarter,
by being the layer that observes across all of them and reasons about what she sees — in
hardware, auditable, and impossible to exploit remotely.
