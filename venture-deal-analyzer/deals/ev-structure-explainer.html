Where the expectancy actually comes from

Both deals run through the same sponsor on the same terms. So the head-to-head reduces to one question: if you strip out the moonshot, is there still a business underneath? For one of them there is.

1. Every dollar of expected value, traced to its source

Expected value is just each outcome's payoff multiplied by its probability, summed. Breaking that sum apart shows which outcomes are actually carrying the number.

Notice the trap in reading this chart: Quaise's tail contributes 0.30 of 1.08 (28%), and DexMat's contributes 0.60 of 1.65 (36%). By that measure DexMat looks more tail-dependent. That measure is wrong, and here is the one that matters.

2. The test that actually separates them: delete the tail

Set the moonshot probability to zero, redistribute that probability across the remaining outcomes, and ask what's left. This is the honest question, because a 3–4% probability estimate is the least knowable number in either model — nobody can defend 3% over 1.5% with real evidence.

DexMat without any moonshot: 1.09x — still above water. The Zoltek-class specialty exit is a 26%-probability outcome that already pays 1.75x net. You are underwritten by a business that exists: ~$400K of revenue growing 2x, 50+ paying customers, product shipping today. The copper-parity tail is upside you aren't paying for.

Quaise without the moonshot: 0.81x — a 19% loss. Its two non-failure branches are a 24%-probability IP/pivot at 1.30x and a 9% commercial success at 4.5x. Together they don't reach break-even. Everything above water comes from a 3% branch.

3. How much of the tail do you need just to break even?

Quaise's whole case rests on a 3% estimate being right when 2.1% is the break-even line. Nobody — not the sponsor, not the panel, not me — can distinguish 3% from 2% from 1.5% for an event like "millimeter-wave drilling reaches commercial depth with survivable completions and lists above Fervo's peak." The margin is inside the noise of the estimate.

4. And the tail is worse than it looks, because the two variables aren't independent

My original tree made an error the red team caught: it treated the probability the tail fires and the size of the tail as separate inputs. They aren't. Both are driven by the same thing — the state of the geothermal and climate-tech listing market in 2029–33.

If that window is open and hot, Quaise is both more likely to get financed all the way to an exit and more likely to exit at a rich multiple. If it's shut, the company is less likely to reach an exit and any exit is cheaper. The good case and the good price arrive together; so do the bad ones. That correlation removes the diversification you get from assuming "low probability but high payoff" are independent draws. Applying it: 1.90x → 1.15x → 0.94x. Below water without forming any view on drilling physics. Fervo, the comp the whole case leans on, is already 21% below its debut.

5. Why the shared sponsor drops out of the comparison — but not the decision

Both vehicles are Delta4 Capital SPVs: same manager, same 2% one-time plus 20% carry, same absent hurdle, same ~$887k of lifetime raises behind them, same missing Form ADV. In a head-to-head, a factor identical on both sides cancels — it cannot tell you which to pick.

It emphatically does not cancel for the question should I invest at all, or how much. Two identical faulty brakes don't help you choose between two cars; they might still tell you to walk. And if you hold both, you don't have two positions — you have one manager bet expressed twice, in illiquid vehicles you can't redeem from.

The practical difference, in one line. DexMat's expectancy rests on claims diligence can settle — is the offtake real, is the counterparty named, are the volumes binding. Quaise's rests on a 3% probability estimate that no amount of diligence can sharpen. Prefer the bet whose crux is a fact you can go get.
