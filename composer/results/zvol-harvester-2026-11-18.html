Is the ZVOL harvester worth its capital?

Study run 2026-11-18 on data to the 11-17 close. Pre-registered, then checked by two independent reviewers whose corrections are applied here. Report-only: nothing was traded or changed.

Short answer: your instinct is closer to right than the earlier "keep" studies said. It is not losing money, but outside two strong stretches it has earned about what T-bills earn, on an edge that depends on being filled at the close in a very small fund. Moving its money into KMLM would probably raise return and would certainly deepen drawdowns; that is a risk choice the data cannot make for you.
  • Live, it is about level with T-bills. Composer shows HARV at -$363 since July. One ZVOL payout of $1,193 landed in account cash, outside the symphony. Counting it, HARV made +$830 against +$446 for T-bills over 83 sessions. That gap is inside the noise.
  • Your "dog" read has a base rate behind it. From 2010 to 2019 the rule lost 1.8% a year net for ten years. Its return sits in 2007–2009 and 2020–2025, and it was picked on the second of those. A flat year like 2026 is the normal case; 2023–2025 was the outlier.
  • The timing is real but fragile. It is the familiar bounce after an oversold close, and it beats shuffled versions of itself in every era. Acted on one close late, the 2006–2023 return falls from +8.9% to -0.1% a year. At 10 bps a trade it falls to +2.0%. Live ZVOL fills have been a median 13 bps worse than the close.
  • It has not been shown to be worse than cash. At its 2023–2026 pace, swapping it for T-bills lowers book return by 4.2 points [1.9 to 6.7] a year. At its long-run pace the difference is -1.9 points [-4.1, +0.4], which cannot be told from zero. On HARV's $37.5k that is about $7,000 a year over bills at the recent pace, $3,000 at the long-run pace, and -$800 at the 2010–2019 pace.
  • Moving HARV into the KMLM symphony is a risk trade. With the switcher held to its live pace the book earns +13.9% [+1.9%, +30.4%] more a year, and the worst drawdown is +4.6 pts [+2.9, +9.6] deeper (nearer +10 points at the switcher's live volatility). Live since July it would have made +32.1% against +27.0%, with a 16.4% drawdown against 10.5%. By the rule fixed in advance this is inconclusive: the return test passes, the drawdown test fails.
  • No refinement earned a recommendation. Term-structure, variance-premium, VVIX and credit variables did not clear the bar set in advance; only SPY's 2-day RSI, the rule's own trigger, did.

2. Why the app and the price chart both read worse than the truth

ZVOL since launch: price against total return

Indexed to 100 on 2023-04-19. The fund has paid $0.485 a share every month since January 2024.

  • A price chart of ZVOL is wrong by design. Price is down 57% since launch; total return is up 56%. Anything that uses price reads each monthly payout as a loss.
  • Composer's symphony curve can lose a payout for good. When the rule sells ZVOL between the ex-date and the pay date, the cash goes to the account and the symphony's curve never gets it back. That has happened once (08-19) and may happen again tomorrow.
  • Held every day, ZVOL has been poor since 2024: +9.3%, −10.7% and +5.1% by year, after +52% in 2023. The rule holds it on about one day in five.

  • One close late and the long-run edge is gone. The trade earns its money in the first session after the signal. A missed or delayed fill is not a small leak; it removes the trade. The IBKR mirror's two blocked ZVOL orders are exactly this case.
  • Cost per trade matters more than anything else. Break-even on the 2006–2023 path is about 11 bps a trade.
  • Live ZVOL fills against the official close: median 13 bps worse, 14 weighted by size, 16 of 22 adverse. One cent is 15 bps at $6.46, and each payout makes that larger. PULS fills cost about nothing, so priced fund by fund (ZVOL 14 / VXZ 3 / PULS 0) the net return is close to the flat 5 bps case (+10.2% before 2023). With 22 fills this bounds the cost only loosely.

6. "Not on when volatility is around"

9. Risks the backtest does not carry

  • ZVOL itself. A $20.6M fund paying out about 82% of its value a year, almost all return of capital. A payout cut, reverse split or closure looks likely within a year or two. It became "actively managed" in October 2025, though it still tracks −1× mid-term futures (beta 0.93–1.00 by half-year). Its prospectus warns of a total loss in a single day, and it cuts exposure around fiscal quarter-ends.
  • Tail risk is larger than 2023–2026 shows: worst day −10.3% and worst drawdown 32% on the long path, against −5.2% and 10% in the real era.
  • The tick is growing. Every payout lowers the price, so one cent of slippage costs more each month.
  • The same rule is held twice, and HARV has no HYG guard despite its name. Earlier "keep" studies (addenda 13, 16, 34) used a guarded history for it.
  • The IBKR mirror cannot buy ZVOL yet (orders held on 11-16 and 11-17), so the two brokers currently differ on exactly this fund.
  • Size. One entry is about $36k of ZVOL across both symphonies against roughly $0.7M traded a day: about 5% of volume.

11. What this taught us about testing

  1. Measure total return, in dollars, at the account. Price charts and the symphony curve both misread a fund that pays out 7% a month. And check which payouts really left: I first counted PULS payouts that had been credited inside.
  2. Ask where in history the return sits. One average pace hid a flat decade. Sub-periods and calendar years go in every study.
  3. Benchmark against the dumb version: the same funds without timing, a permanent position, cash, and the bare trigger.
  4. Test timing with placebos and a mirror, then test the execution: one close late, and costs from 5 to 30 bps. An edge that dies at lag one is an execution bet.
  5. Compare live fills with the price the backtest assumes, fund by fund.
  6. Haircut both sides. I cut the switcher to its live pace and left the harvester at its best-years pace; the reviewer caught it.
  7. Count the candidates. A rule picked as the best of 41 needs a higher bar than one tested alone.
  8. Name predictors in advance and correct for the count. Three of eight looked good until corrected and tested out of sample.
  9. Judge a sleeve by what it does to the book, in dollars at the real weights, with ranges, and say what pace would flip the answer (the break-even).
  10. Write the decision rule first, and check it can be met. One of mine could not.
  11. Keep the independent review. It reversed two headline numbers and changed the tone of this report.

12. Edges and open questions

  • Candidate edge (the rule's own, now measured on 20 years): after SPY's 2-day RSI closes under 25, the short mid-term position earns about 30 bps the next session against 2 on all days (t −4.9, survives correction, positive out of sample; n 4,749). It needs a fill at that close and a vehicle with a tighter tick than ZVOL. Weaker over non-overlapping 5-day windows (t −2.1).
  • Candidate, not robust: SVXY instead of ZVOL, +7.2 points a year in 2023–2026 [+2.1, +12.4], negative before.
  • Candidate, not significant: dropping the VXZ leg lowers drawdown in all three windows.
  • Open: more live fills (22 is too few to bound the cost); what the rule does if ZVOL closes; whether IBKR will clear ZVOL for the mirror; the HYG guard (rename or add); sweeping stranded payouts back in.
