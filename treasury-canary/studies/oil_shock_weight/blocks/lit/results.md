## Bottom line (for the spec's priors)

- The "10 of 11" claim is real and correctly worded in Hamilton (2011): **"All but one of the 11 postwar recessions were associated with an increase in the price of oil, the single exception being the recession of 1960."** Count = 11 recessions with peaks 1948-11 … 2007-12; it is *not* a "+25%" count — 1953 (+10%), 1957 (+9%) and 1969 (+7%, +8%) are in it. 2020 makes it 10 of 12. Two shocks (2003, 2022) had no recession.
- Out of sample, oil adds little to a curve-based recession classifier (Estrella–Mishkin 1998) and ≤1% MSPE gain to GDP forecasts at h=4, nothing at h=1 (Ravazzolo–Rothman 2013). Kilian–Vigfusson (2017) find the net-oil-price-increase model's conditional fit is 59% *worse* than linear across all NOPI episodes; its recession-episode gains come from 1979Q4 and 2007Q4 only.
- Effect size has shrunk by roughly an order of magnitude with the expenditure share and the exporter transition: 15% global supply loss → −0.3pp US growth today vs −5.6pp in 1980 (Kilian–Plante–Richter, Dallas Fed 2026); post-2010 industrial-production response to +10% oil is ~0 to +1% (Känzig–Stock–Zanotti 2026).
- Petrodollar recycling into Treasuries was always partial and identifiable only in part (HKL 2006), and has weakened: Gulf surpluses ≈ $200bn vs Asia ≈ $1.5T (Setser 2026); Saudi Treasuries fell 41% from early 2020 to $108bn by mid-2023 despite a $163bn 2022 surplus. The live mechanism is now oil→dollar (positive since ~2021; BIS 2023, ECB 2024), not oil→Treasury demand.

## (A) Improvements to Casey's thesis ("oil is a bigger market so it deserves more weight; we are on a petrodollar system")

1. **Weight oil by the exposed expenditure share, not the size of the market it trades in.** Oil & products are 3% of US GDP (8% in 1980) and energy goods 2.7% of PCE (6% in 1980), so a +50% shock is a ~1.5%-of-GDP income transfer today vs ~4% in 1980 (Dallas Fed 2026; FRED Blog 2022).
2. **"Bigger market" now cuts the other way: the US is a net petroleum exporter, so the aggregate transfer is ~zero and the response has flipped sign.** 15% global supply loss → −0.3pp US growth vs −5.6pp under 1980 structure (Kilian–Plante–Richter 2026); post-2010 IP response to +10% oil is ~0 to +1% (Känzig–Stock–Zanotti 2026); employment effects "largely disappeared" since 2010 (Boston Fed 2026).
3. **Score the shock's source, not just its size.** Supply-driven shocks depress activity after a lag while demand-driven rises coincide with expansions (Kilian 2009 AER; Baumeister–Hamilton 2019 AER), which is why 2003–08 and 2021 rose without immediate recession — the channel should read IGREA / the narrative list before grading red.
4. **Use the net-oil-price-increase form, but expect it to be a 1979/2008 story.** Hamilton 1996/2003's 3-yr NOPI is the published trigger; Kilian–Vigfusson 2017 find its conditional fit 59% worse than linear across all NOPI episodes and its recession gains confined to 1979Q4 and 2007Q4, i.e. it earns its keep only when a Volcker-scale tightening or a credit crisis is also present.
5. **Demand incremental value over the curve, because the published OOS record is thin.** Beyond one quarter the curve alone beats every tested combination (Estrella–Mishkin 1998) and oil's real-time OOS gain for GDP growth is ≤1% MSPE at h=4 and nil at h=1, declining monotonically in real time (Ravazzolo–Rothman 2013) — so the prior for ΔAUC is small and the spec's interval-based decision rule is the right bar.
6. **Estimate oil jointly with the Fed channel; the anchor episodes are confounded.** BGW 1997 attribute 2/3–3/4 of the post-shock output loss to the systematic funds-rate response, Hamilton–Herrera 2004 show this is lag-length dependent, and Kilian–Lewis 2011 / K–V 2017 find no direct Fed response to oil — 1973, 1979 and 2022 all arrived with tightening, so specs (d)/(e) are load-bearing, not robustness.
7. **Relocate the petrodollar mechanism from "Treasury demand" to "dollar strength".** Identifiable oil-exporter Treasury buying was a minority even in 2002–06 ($107bn of ~$485bn; HKL 2006), oil exporters were <1/4 of safe-asset inflows in 2003–07 (IFDP 1014), Saudi Treasuries fell 41% from 2020 to mid-2023 despite a $163bn 2022 surplus, and Gulf surpluses (~$200bn) are now dwarfed by Asia's (~$1.5T; Setser 2026) — whereas oil→USD turned positive around 2021 (BIS 2023), so H4b, not H4a, carries the thesis.
8. **Test recycling on all US securities and SWF flows, not Treasuries, and expect a weak sign.** In 2022 Saudi Arabia bought $52.5bn and the UAE $34.8bn of US securities (Weiss 2023) while their Treasury lines fell — the money arrives as equities/private assets via PIF/ADIA/KIA and offshore custodians, which TIC "Treasuries by country" cannot see (custody centres held $1.1T).
